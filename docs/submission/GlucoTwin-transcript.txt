00:00 | Introduction and scope
This is GlucoTwin, presented for J R Deva Dattan of Team dev at Army Institute of Technology, Pune. GlucoTwin is a research workspace for inspecting a two-hour glucose event estimate. Every patient you will see in the app is fictional. It is not intended for clinical decisions.  The question behind this prototype is simple: when a glucose reading changes, can a reviewer see what information produced the estimate and whether that information is still usable? The interface brings the current snapshot, model output, and its evidence into the same review flow. This is a proposed clinician-facing workflow. We haven't conducted clinician interviews or a usability study.  I'll show the working replay first, including a false positive and a missed episode. Then I'll separate the synthetic engineering results from a small retrospective benchmark on public research data. The real benchmark exposes a substantial false-alert burden and only a very small difference between fused and glucose-only models. A useful research demonstration should make three kinds of evidence easy to distinguish. The first is software behavior: whether the application performs the operations its interface promises. The second is statistical performance on a held out dataset. The third is evidence that the workflow actually helps clinicians or patients. This project supplies a working software prototype and a limited statistical study. It does not supply the third kind of evidence. That distinction shapes the entire presentation. We will look at both favorable and unfavorable cases, and we will keep the false alert burden visible alongside successful warnings. The goal is inspectability. A reviewer should be able to determine the forecast origin, the inputs, the event definition, and the limitations without needing to guess. All names and glucose traces in the interactive application are synthetic fixtures. This presentation uses a stock computer generated narration voice and captions. It does not reproduce or impersonate the participant's voice.

01:46 | Exact outcome
Our target is two consecutive readings strictly above 180 milligrams per deciliter in the next 120 minutes. Samples arrive every 15 minutes. Two readings span 15 minutes; we do not describe that as 30 minutes above range.  A current reading above 180 suppresses a new-event estimate, even if it is only a single high reading. That is a conservative project choice. Missing future samples make the outcome unknown, rather than negative. These choices are part of the benchmark definition. The threshold has time-in-range literature behind it, but this particular episode protocol is ours. It is not an emergency definition or a treatment trigger. Consider the origin at twelve o'clock. A value measured at twelve o'clock belongs to the information available to the model. It is not one of the future outcome readings. The future window starts with twelve fifteen and ends with fourteen hundred. Suppose the readings at thirteen fifteen and thirteen thirty are both above one hundred and eighty. They form a positive event for this origin. If only the thirteen fifteen reading is above the threshold, with both neighboring readings below it, the same origin is negative under this protocol. A reading exactly equal to one hundred and eighty is not strictly above the threshold. These boundary conditions matter because small changes in labeling can change reported performance. They also matter when explaining why a warning was counted as false. We do not retrospectively redefine the outcome to make a selected example look better.

03:07 | Data separation and timing
There are two evidence streams. The browser runs a model trained on a deterministic synthetic generator. That generator uses static patient context by design. Better fusion results on it demonstrate that the software can combine the inputs; they cannot establish a clinical benefit.  The separate ShanghaiT2DM feasibility benchmark covers 100 adults and 109 recording sessions. We retain every session for a person in one split. Its static inputs are age and source-recorded gender. We assume these were available at enrollment, but their exact prospective timing isn't independently verified.  The source paper allows some laboratory measurements months before or after the sensor recording. We therefore exclude all undated labs, along with treatment history and outcome-derived summary fields. Real data stay outside the public app and repository. The browser's fictional HbA1c fixture is dated before replay and has no connection to a Shanghai participant. The distinction between the two evidence streams also determines what may be inferred from a comparison. Synthetic data are valuable for controlled engineering checks. They let us exercise reproducible histories, known failure states, and code paths without exposing a real person's records. But a relationship written into the generator can be learned by a model without proving that it exists with the same strength in clinical practice. The real benchmark provides a separate feasibility check, rather than a hidden replacement for the app's model. The two pipelines have different features and history requirements. Their percentages should not be exchanged. Grouping all recording sessions for the same person prevents a person's second session from appearing in a test split while their first session was used for training. This removes one important leakage route, although it cannot resolve every concern about retrospective data quality or population representativeness.

Only after those checks should you inspect the model output. 06:29 | Replay and observed outcome
Advancing fifteen minutes adds one observation. The current value and last-hour statistics change, so the event estimate changes too. The future actual trace doesn't appear early. Reset and the time slider make this demonstration repeatable, although moving backwards is a replay convenience rather than a clinical workflow. This is the selected successful-warning illustration. It shows one outcome that followed a warning. It does not represent the overall success rate. Once the current value is already above range, the interface treats that as an observation and stops presenting it as advance prediction. Demo review: inspected inputs and replay outcome. The replay is deliberately observable. At each step, the current measurement becomes part of the past, the forecast origin advances, and the input summary is recomputed. The presence of future readings inside the fixture file does not make them valid inference inputs. The engineering requirement is to restrict the calculation to the chosen origin. When the replay reaches the first high measurement, a new event estimate is withheld because the current reading is already above the event threshold. That behavior should not be confused with the model changing its mind about the earlier forecast. The earlier forecast had its own origin and its own future window. A review note is merely a local session annotation for this demonstration. Marking a snapshot reviewed does not imply a clinician approved it, that a treatment was recommended, or that any notification was sent to a patient. The distinction between observing, forecasting, and acting remains explicit.

07:53 | False positive and missed episode
This warning is a false positive under our exact label. A single high reading is visible, but the required pair never forms in this window. Changing the event definition after seeing this example would change the task. We keep the definition fixed. This selected case is a genuinely missed synthetic episode. The eligible warning origins stay below the threshold before onset. A low model estimate should never be read as a guarantee that no event will occur. These examples were selected after aggregate evaluation to show behavior. They were not used to train the model or select its threshold. The false positive and missed event make different limitations tangible. A false positive consumes attention even when its displayed score is below fifty percent. An alerting rule can use a low threshold when recall is prioritized, so a warning is not the same as a prediction that an event is more likely than not. Conversely, a low score can precede a real event under the chosen label. The missed example is useful because it prevents the dashboard from presenting a reassuring percentage as certainty. These cases are illustrations selected from held out synthetic examples after aggregate evaluation. They do not provide a new estimate of accuracy. A few visually memorable examples can easily distort intuition, which is why the aggregate metrics later in this walkthrough are essential. The same fixed event definition must be applied to the successful warning, false warning, and missed episode. Otherwise the comparison would be unfair.

09:14 | Missing data and rejected inputs
The prototype withholds its forecast when the feed is stale. Its 30-minute freshness limit is an engineering policy. It isn't a clinical standard. Cold start, invalid units, and a disconnected feed also produce explicit unavailable states. The screen must not turn missing information into a reassuring low percentage.  The interface requires nine observations before showing its forecast. The synthetic training eligibility uses at least five contiguous observations, and the real benchmark requires 24. Those are separate contracts, and the documentation records the difference. We test input order, gaps, units, and export parity in the model and browser code. These scenario controls demonstrate selected failure states; they are not a live sensor integration. An unavailable output is an important result. It tells the reviewer that the system cannot support the requested computation under its input contract. Sensor dropout and disconnection are related but distinct: a connection may appear to exist while its latest usable observation is too old. A cold start means that the necessary history has not yet accumulated. Invalid units mean the numerical value cannot safely be interpreted under the expected contract. The scenario selector makes these states easy to inspect, but it is not proof that the application connects to or validates a physical glucose monitor. The freshness rule, history requirement, and unit checks are engineering choices for this prototype. They need to be documented and tested, and they would need a separate review for a future clinical application. A blank or unavailable state must remain visibly different from a low estimated event score.

10:43 | Architecture and reproducibility
The running app is a React and TypeScript application built with Vite. Browser inference uses exported synthetic logistic coefficients and scaling values. A Python service is not required at runtime. That keeps the demo reproducible without accounts, paid APIs, or patient uploads.  The offline Python pipeline generates synthetic data, makes patient-level splits, fits the scaler and classifier on training people, selects thresholds on validation people, and exports versioned JSON. Browser parity fixtures compare the TypeScript calculation with Python outputs. The real benchmark uses a separate local pipeline and exports aggregate evidence only.  A digital-twin claim also needs care here. This prototype updates a virtual patient state with simulated sensor observations. It has no physical patient connection, no validated physiology simulator, and no autonomous treatment loop. Reproducibility requires more than a screenshot of a good result. The repository needs the preprocessing logic, the split protocol, the model configuration, the selected thresholds, and the exported artifacts used by the interface. The scaler must be fitted on training people rather than the entire dataset. Threshold selection belongs on validation data. Test results should be reported only after those choices have been fixed. Browser parity checks address a different question: whether the exported model is evaluated consistently by TypeScript and Python. They do not validate the statistical assumptions of the model. The architecture keeps runtime inference local to the browser for the synthetic demonstration. The separate retrospective benchmark is an offline computation that publishes aggregate evidence. No real subject trace is necessary for a reviewer to inspect those reported metrics. The term digital twin here describes an updating virtual state, with the limitations shown on the slide.

12:25 | Synthetic evidence
The synthetic evaluation includes 240 people, split 144 for training, 48 for validation, and 48 for testing. The held-out comparison uses the same 30,248 eligible windows. PR-AUC here means average precision, not trapezoidal area under a plotted curve.  Fused PR-AUC is 0.3205 compared with 0.2837 for dynamic-only, 0.1776 for static-only, and 0.0773 for constant persistence scores. The fused patient-bootstrap interval is about 0.2731 to 0.3581. Those correlated windows are not 30,248 independent patients.  The frozen synthetic threshold is 0.15. It produces 944 false alerts over 336 patient-days, about 2.81 per day. It warns 293 of 334 forecastable episodes, with median lead time of 30 minutes and window precision of 27.2%. The episode count and alert burden belong together. Alert thresholds were selected separately for each model, so workload is not matched across models. Average precision summarizes how precision changes as a score threshold varies. Its baseline depends strongly on how common the positive outcome is. That is why prevalence and the common evaluation denominator belong beside the model comparison. Overlapping windows from one person's glucose trace are correlated. Treating every window as an independent patient would exaggerate the amount of independent information in the study. Resampling whole people is one way to reflect this clustering in uncertainty estimates. The alert workload answers another practical question. A model may warn many episodes and still create too many interruptions for a useful workflow. The cooldown prevents every consecutive positive window from being counted as a new alert, but it does not eliminate false alerts. Different models can select different thresholds on validation data, so an apparent sensitivity advantage may come with a different workload. The persistence trajectory comparison is a regression comparison and should remain separate from the event classification result.

14:30 | Real benchmark and uncertainty
The real feasibility study has 60 training, 20 validation, and 20 held-out people. There are 20,687 eligible test windows, with event prevalence about 9.95%. Fused PR-AUC is 0.3422 and dynamic-only is 0.3398. The difference is about 0.0024. Its paired patient-bootstrap interval runs from about minus 0.00248 to plus 0.00758 and includes zero. We have not established a meaningful fusion benefit on these data.  At the validation-selected threshold of 0.20, window precision is 28.9% and recall is 50.5%. The model warns 302 of 326 forecastable episodes, with a 45-minute median lead. It also produces 754 false alerts, or about 2.97 per recorded patient-day. The denominator includes gaps and periods already above range. It is recorded-span time, not verified sensor uptime.  Alerts have a 120-minute cooldown. Episodes and alerts are counted separately, and one alert can warn more than one episode inside its horizon. These details explain why event sensitivity alone would overstate usefulness. The study is retrospective, small, and geographically limited. It cannot validate care in India. The paired interval is especially important. Comparing two separately rounded point estimates can make a tiny difference look more decisive than it is. Here the interval for the difference includes zero. The reasonable interpretation is that this study has not established a meaningful advantage for fusion over dynamic glucose features alone. That conclusion is compatible with both models having discrimination above the constant baseline. It is also compatible with both being unsuitable for clinical use at the measured alert burden. Window recall and episode warning rate are different quantities. Many overlapping opportunities may exist before one episode, and one alert may cover more than one episode in its future horizon. Counting those quantities carefully avoids accidental inflation of success. The denominator for false alerts per day includes the full recorded span, including gaps, rather than confirmed uptime. The result should therefore be read with that exact denominator and not converted into an unsupported sensor reliability claim.

16:43 | Limits, privacy, and next validation
Before clinical use, this would need independent local validation, verified feature timing, clinician review of the event definition and acceptable alert burden, and ethics, privacy, and security review. A larger model alone would not resolve those questions.  The public demo collects no real patient records. The real source files remain outside the repository and recording. We make no claim of regulatory compliance, diagnostic ability, treatment benefit, or safe dosing. The next research question is whether a carefully timed and externally tested model can reduce false alerts while retaining useful advance warnings. Our present results do not answer that. A credible next step would begin with a specified clinical question and a prospective data availability contract. Researchers would need to decide which inputs are genuinely available at the forecast time, which population is represented, and what actions, if any, a reviewer would take after an alert. They would need to establish acceptable alert burden with relevant clinicians rather than selecting a threshold solely because it maximizes a retrospective summary metric. Independent local validation matters because a small geographically limited source cannot stand in for care in India. Privacy and security review also need to cover the actual deployment, access rules, retention policy, and data flows of a future system. None of those requirements are satisfied just by using a synthetic demo. For this entry, the narrower achievement is a working, inspectable research prototype that communicates these limits instead of hiding them.
